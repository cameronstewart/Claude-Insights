# Labour hire exemption in the WA Employment Agents Act 1976

The Western Australian Employment Agents Act 1976 explicitly exempts labour hire companies from its licensing requirements through a carefully crafted statutory provision that has remained unchanged since the Act's inception. While the specific parliamentary debates from 1976 remain inaccessible in digital archives, the legislative text and subsequent government documents reveal clear policy rationales for this distinction.

## The statutory exemption explained

Section 5(3) of the Employment Agents Act 1976 contains the key exemption that distinguishes labour hire from employment agencies. The provision states that a person who "as principal, is the employer responsible for the payment of wages or other lawful obligations to an employee" and provides these employees' services to other persons for temporary tasks based on predetermined rates, without charging fees to the employee, "shall not be deemed by reason only of that fact to be an employment agent."

This legislative language establishes **five critical distinctions** that justify the exemption. First, the labour hire company must be the principal employer bearing full responsibility for wages and employment obligations. Second, it provides the services of its own employees rather than acting as an intermediary. Third, the work must be temporary in nature. Fourth, rates are predetermined between the labour hire company and the host employer. Finally, no fees can be charged to workers.

The policy logic embedded in this statutory framework reflects a fundamental difference in business models. Employment agents operate as intermediaries who match job seekers with employers for a fee, creating a consumer protection concern about potential exploitation of vulnerable job seekers. Labour hire companies, by contrast, maintain ongoing employment relationships with their workers and charge fees to client businesses for services rendered, not to the workers themselves.

## Historical context shaped the regulatory approach

The mid-1970s in Western Australia witnessed a transformative period driven by the mining boom following major resource discoveries in the 1960s. This expansion created unprecedented demand for flexible workforce arrangements, particularly in remote mining regions like the Pilbara where project-based work required scalable employment solutions.

The labour hire industry in 1970s Australia was still relatively nascent, having evolved from clerical "temping agencies" in the 1950s to encompass broader sectors by the 1970s. Unlike the mature industry we see today, labour hire companies of that era often operated as extensions of recruitment agencies, offering temporary staffing as an alternative service to permanent placements. The industry's small scale and distinct operational model likely contributed to the decision to exempt it from employment agent regulation.

**The industrial relations environment of the 1970s** also influenced this regulatory choice. This period saw significant industrial tensions, strong union membership, and ongoing disputes over wages and working conditions. The Fraser Government's approach to industrial relations focused on centralized wage-fixing and reducing strike levels, creating a policy environment that distinguished between different forms of employment services based on their operational characteristics rather than treating them uniformly.

## Policy rationale reveals deliberate regulatory design

The exemption reflects several interconnected policy considerations that remain evident in government documentation. The primary rationale centered on **consumer protection focus** - the Employment Agents Act aimed to protect job seekers from exploitation by agencies charging placement fees. Since labour hire companies employed workers directly and charged client businesses rather than workers, they did not present the same consumer protection risks.

The distinction also recognized **different regulatory needs** between intermediary services and direct employment relationships. Labour hire companies, as actual employers, were already subject to employment law obligations regarding wages, conditions, and worker protections. Adding employment agent licensing requirements would have created regulatory duplication without addressing distinct risks.

Furthermore, the exemption acknowledged the **legitimate business function** of labour hire in providing workforce flexibility for industries with fluctuating demand. In Western Australia's resource-driven economy, this flexibility was particularly valuable for project-based mining operations and seasonal work patterns.

## Persistence of the exemption demonstrates policy consistency

The remarkable consistency of this exemption over nearly five decades provides important insights. A 2015 review of the Employment Agents Act by the Department of Commerce (now the Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety) maintained the labour hire exemption, indicating continued support for the original policy distinction.

This persistence becomes more significant when contrasted with other Australian jurisdictions. Queensland, Victoria, South Australia, and the Australian Capital Territory have all implemented separate labour hire licensing schemes between 2018 and 2021, while Western Australia maintains its original approach alongside New South Wales, Northern Territory, and Tasmania.

**The 2019 Wage Theft Inquiry** marked a potential turning point in Western Australian policy. Led by Tony Beech, former Chief Commissioner of the WA Industrial Relations Commission, the inquiry identified wage theft as particularly prevalent in labour hire arrangements, especially in horticulture. The inquiry's Recommendation 22 saw the WA Government commit "in principle to a labour hire licensing scheme," suggesting recognition that the original exemption may no longer adequately address modern labour hire practices.

## Documentary evidence reveals policy evolution

While the original 1976 parliamentary debates remain inaccessible in digital form - representing a significant gap in the historical record - subsequent government documents illuminate the policy thinking. The Act's current administration explicitly lists labour hire companies among exempted categories, alongside newspaper publications, disability employment service providers, and government departments.

The administrative guidance from the Department of Energy, Mines, Industry Regulation and Safety confirms that approximately 590 licensed employment agents operate in Western Australia, with labour hire companies remaining outside this system. The low complaint levels (averaging 15 per year) suggest the regulatory framework has functioned adequately for its intended purpose, though this may not capture issues specific to labour hire arrangements.

## Contemporary relevance and future directions

The historical exemption of labour hire companies from the Employment Agents Act 1976 reflects a deliberate policy choice based on operational distinctions that made sense in the 1970s context. The exemption recognized that labour hire companies operated as employers rather than intermediaries, with different risk profiles and regulatory needs.

However, the evolution of the labour hire industry from a small, specialized service to a major employment sector employing hundreds of thousands of workers nationally has created new policy challenges. The 2019 Wage Theft Inquiry's findings about exploitation in labour hire arrangements suggest that the original rationale for exemption - that labour hire companies don't present the same consumer protection risks as employment agents - may need reconsideration in the modern context.

Western Australia's commitment in principle to a labour hire licensing scheme indicates recognition that the regulatory distinction established in 1976 may require updating. Rather than bringing labour hire under the Employment Agents Act, the proposed approach would create a parallel regulatory framework specifically designed for the modern labour hire industry's characteristics and risks.

This policy evolution demonstrates how historical regulatory decisions, while appropriate for their time, must adapt to changing industry practices and emerging forms of worker vulnerability. The persistence of the 1976 exemption for nearly five decades testifies to the original policy's coherence, while current reform discussions acknowledge that effective regulation must evolve with the industries it governs.
